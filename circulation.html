1. Blood components & adaptations

Blood is a tissue: red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets are suspended in liquid plasma.
Exam answer essentials
  • An adaptation answer needs a link: no nucleus → more space for haemoglobin → more oxygen can be carried. Platelets are fragments, not whole cells.
ComponentFunction & adaptation
Red blood cellsCarry oxygen using haemoglobin. Biconcave shape gives a large surface-area-to-volume ratio; no nucleus gives more space for haemoglobin.
White blood cellsDefend against pathogens: some engulf them; others produce specific antibodies or antitoxins. They have a nucleus and may change shape.
PlateletsSmall cell fragments involved in clotting → reduce blood loss and entry of pathogens.
PlasmaCarries the cells, nutrients such as glucose and amino acids, hormones, carbon dioxide and urea. It also distributes heat.
  • When evaluating blood products or transfusions, weigh the benefit against possible risks, including immune reactions to incompatible blood and infection transmission; screening and compatibility checks reduce risks.

2. Arteries, veins & capillaries

Exam answer essentials
  • Define vessels by direction relative to the heart, not oxygen content. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood.
  • In comparisons, say thicker muscle / elastic layers and narrower lumen, rather than “bigger”. Blood vessels do not have cell walls. (June 2023 Higher, Q02.4.)
VesselDirection / roleStructure → function
ArteryCarries blood away from the heart.Thick muscular and elastic walls withstand high pressure; elastic recoil helps maintain blood flow. Relatively narrow lumen.
VeinCarries blood towards the heart at lower pressure.Thinner walls and a large lumen reduce resistance. Valves help prevent backflow.
CapillaryExchanges substances between blood and tissues.Wall only one cell thick → short diffusion distance. Extensive networks provide a large exchange area.
Blood flow rate = volume of blood ÷ time
  • Example: 150 cm³ in 30 s → 5 cm³/s. Convert units before comparing rates.
  • Oxygen and glucose move from blood to cells; carbon dioxide moves from respiring cells into the blood.

3. The heart & double circulation

Double circulation: blood passes through the heart twice in one complete circuit — once for the lungs and once for the body.
Exam answer essentials
  • The left ventricle needs a thicker muscle wall to generate the higher pressure needed to send blood around the whole body.
  • The coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself; they do not carry blood from the heart to the lungs.
  • Atria receive blood; ventricles pump it out. Valves prevent backflow and the septum separates the two sides.
  • The right ventricle pumps to the nearby lungs at lower pressure; the left pumps to the whole body at higher pressure.
  • Gas exchange at the lungs adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide; oxygen is delivered to body cells for respiration.
  • The natural pacemaker is a group of cells in the right atrium controlling resting heart rate. An artificial pacemaker supplies electrical impulses to correct an irregular rhythm.
  • Double circulation permits a strong blood supply to the body while keeping pressure through the delicate lung capillaries lower.

4. Coronary heart disease

Coronary heart disease (CHD) occurs when fatty deposits build up inside the coronary arteries and narrow them, reducing blood flow to the heart muscle.
Exam answer essentials
  • Explain a chain: narrower artery → less oxygen reaches heart muscle → less aerobic respiration → less energy released → weaker contraction. Naming a heart attack alone does not explain the mechanism. (June 2023 Higher, Q02.8.)
  • The heart muscle needs a continuous supply of oxygen and glucose for respiration. Reduced supply may cause pain and impair its pumping.
  • A clot can block a coronary artery completely, cutting off blood to part of the muscle and causing a heart attack.
  • For data questions, name both variables and the direction: as artery blockage increases, blood flow decreases. Do not claim a relationship is proportional unless the data support that.

5. Treatments: benefits & risks

Exam answer essentials
  • Pair each treatment with how it helps: stent → keeps artery open; statin → reduces blood cholesterol. (June 2023 Higher, Q02.9.)
  • Evaluate using the patient’s circumstances: include a benefit, a relevant risk or limitation, and a justified choice.
TreatmentBenefitRisk / limitation
StentMesh tube keeps a narrowed coronary artery open → improves blood flow to heart muscle.Procedure has risks; clots can form, so medicines may be needed.
StatinLowers blood cholesterol → slows fatty deposits and reduces cardiovascular risk.Long-term regular use; possible side effects; does not instantly remove an existing blockage.
Replacement valveReplaces a valve that fails to open properly or leaks, improving effective blood flow.Mechanical valves last well but may require lifelong anticoagulants; biological valves may wear out sooner. Both involve surgery.
Donor heart / heart-and-lung transplantReplaces a severely failing organ with a functioning one.Limited donors, major surgery and risk of rejection; immunosuppressants increase infection risk.
Artificial heartMaintains circulation while awaiting a transplant or allows the heart to rest during recovery.Risks of infection or blood clots; requires power and specialist care.
  • A faulty valve that opens poorly restricts flow; a leaking valve allows backflow. The heart may need to work harder to maintain circulation.
  • An artificial pacemaker controls rhythm; an artificial heart pumps blood. They solve different problems.

6. Health & interactions between diseases

Health includes physical and mental well-being. Communicable diseases can spread between organisms; non-communicable diseases cannot.
Exam answer essentials
  • An interaction needs a link between conditions, not two unrelated disease names. A risk factor raises likelihood; it does not make a disease inevitable.
  • Diet, stress and life circumstances can affect mental and physical health as well as disease.
  • A weakened immune system increases susceptibility to infections.
  • Some viruses can trigger cancers by affecting infected cells.
  • Immune responses initially triggered by a pathogen may lead to allergies, such as rashes or asthma.
  • Severe physical illness may contribute to depression or other mental illness.

7. Lifestyle risks & interpreting evidence

A risk factor is linked to an increased chance of disease. It may be a lifestyle choice or a substance in the body or environment.
Exam answer essentials
  • Correlation does not establish causation. Look for a plausible mechanism, representative samples and other factors that could explain the pattern.
Risk factorPossible effect / mechanism
Diet, smoking & little exerciseCan increase cardiovascular risk; exercise and a balanced diet help reduce risk. Smoking damages blood vessels.
ObesityRaises the risk of type 2 diabetes; body cells may respond less effectively to insulin.
AlcoholCan damage the liver and affect brain function.
SmokingDamages lungs and raises lung-cancer risk. Carbon monoxide reduces the blood’s oxygen-carrying capacity.
Smoking / alcohol in pregnancyCan affect development of the unborn baby; smoking reduces oxygen delivery, while alcohol can damage development.
Carcinogens, including ionising radiationCan damage DNA and increase the risk of cancer.
  • Disease often results from several interacting factors, including inherited susceptibility. Some risk factors have a demonstrated causal mechanism; others are only associated.
  • Costs include pain, reduced quality of life and lost earnings for individuals; caring responsibilities, healthcare costs and lost productivity for communities and nations.
  • Compare rates or percentages when group sizes differ. Use representative samples, consider sample size and check factors such as age before making a conclusion.
  • For scatter graphs, describe positive, negative or no correlation. For continuous grouped data use a histogram; for categories use a bar chart. Read axes, units and scales carefully.

8. Benign & malignant tumours

Cancer involves changes in cells causing uncontrolled growth and division.
Exam answer essentials
  • The defining difference is invasion and spread, not simply tumour size. Explain how malignant cells form secondary tumours.
Benign tumourMalignant tumour
Abnormal cells remain in one area, usually contained within a membrane.Cancer cells invade nearby tissues.
Does not invade other tissues or spread to distant organs. It may still cause harm by pressure on nearby structures.Cells can travel in the blood and form secondary tumours elsewhere.
  • Both lifestyle / environmental factors and inherited genetic factors can affect cancer risk. Not everyone exposed to a risk factor develops cancer.
