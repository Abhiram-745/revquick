1. Vaccination & a faster immune response

  • A vaccine introduces dead or inactive forms of a pathogen, containing its antigens, without causing the disease in this GCSE model.
  • White blood cells / lymphocytes produce antibodies specific to those antigens. Memory cells remain.
  • If the same pathogen enters later, specific antibodies are produced more rapidly and in larger quantities; pathogens are destroyed before they cause illness.
  • Vaccinating a large proportion of the population reduces transmission: fewer susceptible people mean fewer opportunities for the pathogen to spread.
  • Evaluate vaccination using evidence of protection, reduced spread, possible side effects and the risk from the disease. Use the information supplied.
Exam answer essentials
  • The vaccine does not contain antibodies in this explanation. Name white blood cells, state specificity, then the faster later response. (2022 Higher examiner report, Q07.6.)

  • Do not assume every antibiotic treats every bacterium. Selection depends on the pathogen and its susceptibility.
Exam answer essentials
  • For “why not antibiotics for a viral infection?”, identify the virus and state that antibiotics act on bacteria.

3. How antibiotic resistance spreads

  1. Variation: a mutation can give some bacteria resistance. Resistance is present before selection; antibiotics do not deliberately teach bacteria to adapt.
  2. Selection: the antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant bacteria survive.
  3. Reproduction: survivors reproduce rapidly, passing resistance to descendants.
  4. The resistant strain becomes more common and may spread between people, making the disease harder to treat.
  • Reducing unnecessary antibiotic use reduces selection for resistant strains. Antibiotics should be used as prescribed.
  • Use data to compare proportions or numbers of resistant bacteria; do not assume every survivor is resistant without supporting evidence.
Exam answer essentials
  • Bacteria become resistant, not the patient. Explain survival and reproduction, rather than writing only “mutation”.

5. Testing drugs: safety, efficacy & dose

Toxicity: harmful effects. Efficacy: how well a drug works. Optimum dose: a dose giving effective treatment while limiting harm.
  1. Preclinical testing: test on cells and tissues in the laboratory, then live animals, to assess toxicity and efficacy.
  2. Healthy volunteers: clinical trials begin with very low doses to check safety and side effects.
  3. Patients: test whether the drug treats the disease, and determine an optimum dose. Further trials investigate different doses.
  4. Compare a drug group with a suitable control / placebo group. A placebo lacks the active drug, helping separate drug effects from expectations.
  5. Use a double-blind trial: neither participants nor the researchers assessing them know who receives drug or placebo. This reduces bias.
  6. Results undergo peer review: other scientists scrutinise the methods and evidence before publication.
Exam answer essentials
  • Do not say a trial “proves it is safe”. It provides evidence about effectiveness and risks under the tested conditions.
  • Match the term to the purpose: placebo = comparison; double blind = less bias; peer review = scrutiny by scientists.

6. Making monoclonal antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies are produced from a single clone of cells. They are identical and specific to one binding site on one protein antigen.
  1. Expose a mouse to the antigen to stimulate lymphocytes to produce the required antibody. Collect antibody-producing lymphocytes.
  2. Fuse a lymphocyte with a tumour cell. The lymphocyte makes the antibody; the tumour cell can divide repeatedly.
  3. The resulting hybridoma cell both divides and produces the antibody.
  4. Select one hybridoma producing the required antibody and clone it. Many identical hybridoma cells are produced.
  5. All cells in the clone produce the same specific antibody. Grow them to produce large amounts.
  6. Collect and purify the antibodies before use.
Exam answer essentials
  • Fuse cells, not antibodies. Clone the hybridoma, not the antibody.
  • If the question begins after hybridoma production, start at cloning; do not spend your answer repeating fusion. Include identical cells → same antibody → collection and purification. (2023 Higher, Q04.5.)
AQA 4.3.2.2 · Biology only · HT only

7. Applying monoclonal antibodies: pregnancy tests

Pregnancy-test antibodies detect the hormone hCG. Their binding sites are complementary to hCG. The exam supplies details of the particular test; apply those details.
  1. Urine moves along a test strip. If hCG is present, it binds to mobile antibodies carrying a coloured label.
  2. At the test line, fixed antibodies capture hCG, retaining the labelled complexes. Enough coloured label accumulates to make a visible line.
  3. A separate control line should appear to show the sample has moved through and the test is working. Its chemistry is described in the question.
  4. Read the result using the test’s instructions. In this common two-line model, test + control is positive; control only is negative; no control makes the result invalid.
Exam answer essentials
  • The control line shows the test has worked; it does not indicate pregnancy. (2023 Higher, Q04.7.)
  • For a missing line, give evidence + mechanism: the labelled antibodies did not reach or bind at that line, so colour did not accumulate. Use the stated design.

8. Uses, targeted treatment & evaluation

  • Diagnosis: detect a specific hormone, pathogen or other chemical; laboratory tests can measure hormones and other chemicals in blood.
  • Research: attach a fluorescent dye to an antibody. It binds to a chosen molecule, letting researchers locate it in a cell or tissue.
  • Cancer treatment: attach a toxic drug, radioactive substance or growth-inhibiting chemical to the antibody.
  • The antibody binds to a specific antigen on cancer cells because their shapes are complementary. It carries the attached treatment to those cells.
  • Targeting may reduce damage to other cells, but does not guarantee no side effects. Monoclonal antibodies have caused more side effects than initially expected and are less widely used than first hoped in the AQA account.
When asked to evaluateWhat to explain
Potential benefitSpecific targeting may improve treatment / reduce damage elsewhere; use effectiveness data.
Possible disadvantageSide effects, cost and limitations shown by the evidence; do not invent a zero-risk treatment.
Ethical issueAnimal use, potential harm, informed consent and possible patient benefit. Weigh reasons rather than simply calling it “wrong”.
Exam answer essentials
  • A treatment explanation needs complementary antigen → antibody binds → attached treatment delivered. “It targets cancer” alone misses the mechanism. (2022 Higher, Q07.8.)
