Cell structure & specialisation

Meet the cell types, learn what each structure does, and explain how specialised cells are adapted.

1. Eukaryotes & prokaryotes

Eukaryotic cells have genetic material enclosed in a nucleus. Animal and plant cells are eukaryotic. Prokaryotic cells, such as bacteria, have genetic material that is not enclosed in a nucleus.
FeatureAnimal / plant cellsBacterial cells
Genetic materialDNA in a nucleusSingle DNA loop free in cytoplasm; may also contain small circular plasmids
Shared structuresCell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomesCell membrane, cytoplasm and ribosomes
Other structuresMitochondria; photosynthetic plant cells also have chloroplastsCell wall; no mitochondria or chloroplasts
Relative sizeUsually much largerMuch smaller than eukaryotic cells
Mark scheme focusCompare the same feature on both sides: “Bacterial DNA is not enclosed in a nucleus, whereas animal-cell DNA is.” Do not claim that bacteria have no DNA or no cell membrane. Use the features shown in an unfamiliar cell image rather than assuming every eukaryote has an animal-cell structure (AQA June 2023, Q05.2).

2. Sub-cellular structures

Each structure contributes to a cell’s function. Learn a precise function for each one, then use it to explain unfamiliar cells.

StructureFunction you should know
NucleusContains genetic material and controls the cell’s activities.
CytoplasmWhere most chemical reactions occur; enzymes control these reactions.
Cell membraneControls the movement of substances into and out of the cell.
MitochondriaSite of aerobic respiration, which releases energy for cellular processes.
RibosomesSite of protein synthesis.
Cell wallMade of cellulose in plants and algae; strengthens and supports the cell.
ChloroplastsSite of photosynthesis; contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy.
Permanent vacuoleContains cell sap; helps maintain turgor and support the plant cell.
Plasmids in bacteriaSmall circular DNA molecules carrying additional genes, which may provide useful characteristics such as antibiotic resistance.

Most animal cells have the first five structures. Plant cells have these too, plus a cellulose cell wall and permanent vacuole; photosynthetic plant cells also have chloroplasts. Root hair cells normally lack chloroplasts because they are underground. Bacterial cell walls are not made of cellulose; cellulose walls are a plant and algal feature.

Mark scheme focusThe membrane controls entry and exit; the wall supports the cell. Say energy is released by respiration, not created by mitochondria. Ribosomes make proteins, not energy. AQA June 2022 Paper 1 Higher, Q01.1, distinguishes respiration / energy release from energy creation.

3. Specialised cells

A specialised cell has structures adapted to carry out a particular function.
Cell & functionAdaptation → why it helps
Sperm: fertilise an eggTail → movement towards the egg. Many mitochondria → energy released for movement. Enzymes in the head → help penetrate the egg’s outer layers.
Nerve: transmit electrical impulsesLong axon → carries impulses over long distances. Branched ends → connect to other cells. Insulating sheath → speeds transmission.
Muscle: contract for movementContractile protein fibres → shorten the cell. Many mitochondria → energy released for contraction.
Root hair: absorb water and mineral ionsLong projection → large surface area for absorption. Many mitochondria → energy for active transport of mineral ions.
Xylem: carry water and mineral ions; support the plantDead, hollow cells joined into continuous tubes → unobstructed water transport. Lignified walls → strength and support.
Phloem: transport dissolved sugarsSieve tubes with perforated end plates → allow sap to pass. Companion cells contain many mitochondria → provide energy for transport processes.
Mark scheme focusUse feature → effect → function: “The root hair gives a large surface area, increasing absorption of water and mineral ions.” Naming a feature alone may not earn an explanation mark.
AQA 4.1.1.4

4. Differentiation

Differentiation is the process by which a cell changes to become specialised for its function.

Animals

Most types of animal cell differentiate at an early stage. Many mature cells lose the ability to differentiate. In mature animals, cell division mainly replaces and repairs cells.

Plants

Many types of plant cell retain the ability to differentiate throughout life. Cells in meristems continue dividing and can produce specialised cells for growth.

As cells differentiate, they develop different sub-cellular structures. For example, a muscle cell develops many mitochondria and contractile fibres. This allows it to perform its specific function.

Mark scheme focusKeep the two processes distinct: mitosis increases the number of cells; differentiation produces specialised cells.

5. Size & units

You should compare cell and sub-cellular sizes, use standard form, and understand differences in orders of magnitude.

1 cm = 10 mm   ·   1 mm = 1,000 µm   ·   1 µm = 1,000 nm

For smaller units, multiply; for larger units, divide. For example, 0.025 mm = 25 µm = 25,000 nm. In standard form, 0.025 mm = 2.5 × 10−2 mm.

An order of magnitude is a factor of 10. A 20 µm cell is 10 times the length of a 2 µm bacterium: a difference of one order of magnitude. Typical values are context, not fixed sizes for every cell.

When estimating the size of a structure in an image, compare it with a known scale or cell size. If a nucleus is about one-quarter the width of a 40 µm cell, estimate its width as 10 µm. Estimate areas using appropriate shapes, such as πr² for a roughly circular outline; state the simplification.

Mark scheme focusRead the question’s units before calculating. Keep the numerical value and unit together; do not round intermediate steps unnecessarily.
