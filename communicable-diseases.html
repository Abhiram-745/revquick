1. Pathogens & how they cause disease

Communicable diseases are infectious diseases that can spread between individuals. A pathogen is a microorganism that causes disease.
PathogenHow it causes diseaseAQA examples
BacteriumReproduces rapidly in the body; may produce toxins that damage tissues and make us feel ill.Salmonella; gonorrhoea
VirusReproduces inside living cells, damaging cells.Measles; HIV; tobacco mosaic virus
FungusCan infect tissues, damaging them.Rose black spot
ProtistA eukaryotic microorganism; some species are parasites.The protist that causes malaria
Exam answer essentials
  • Name the pathogen type, then explain its effect. “Germs make you ill” does not explain how.
  • Pathogen is the disease-causing organism; the disease is the illness it causes.

2. Spread & reducing transmission

RouteExampleWhy the precaution works
Air / dropletsMeasles from coughs or sneezesCovering coughs and reducing contact limit exposure; vaccination reduces susceptible people.
Contaminated water / foodSalmonella from contaminated foodGood hygiene, cooking and safe water reduce the number of pathogens swallowed.
Direct contact / body fluidsGonorrhoea; HIVCondoms reduce exchange of infected fluids; sterile needles prevent infected blood transfer.
VectorMosquitoes carry the malaria protistNets prevent bites; preventing mosquito breeding reduces the number of vectors.
  • Hygiene: handwashing and cleaning reduce transfer of pathogens.
  • Isolation: separating an infected individual reduces contact and therefore transmission.
  • Destroying vectors and vaccination can reduce disease spread. Choose a measure that fits the route in the question.
Exam answer essentials
  • A prevention answer needs action → reduced transmission. Antibiotics treat some infections; they are not a substitute for preventing exposure.

3. Measles, HIV, Salmonella & gonorrhoea

DiseaseWhat you need to add to the diagram
MeaslesFever and a red skin rash; can cause serious complications and be fatal. Vaccination protects against infection.
HIVAn initial flu-like illness may occur. Antiretroviral drugs control HIV reproduction. If not controlled, HIV damages immune cells and may lead to AIDS, when the body cannot effectively fight other infections or cancers.
SalmonellaFood poisoning: fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea. Bacteria and their toxins cause symptoms. Poultry are vaccinated in the UK to control spread.
GonorrhoeaA sexually transmitted bacterial disease: thick yellow/green discharge and pain when urinating. Antibiotics treat it, but resistant strains limit treatment; condoms reduce spread.
Exam answer essentials
  • HIV is the virus; AIDS is the later condition. Do not say HIV spreads through ordinary social contact.
  • Use antiretroviral drugs for HIV and antibiotics for bacterial infections. Gonorrhoea has strains resistant to penicillin.

4. Malaria: pathogen versus vector

  • The protist is the pathogen. A vector carries a pathogen between hosts; here, it is the mosquito.
  • The malaria pathogen has a life cycle involving mosquitoes and humans. An infected mosquito transfers the protist when it bites.
  • Malaria causes recurrent episodes of fever and can be fatal.
  • Mosquito nets reduce bites. Preventing mosquitoes from breeding, for example by managing stagnant water, reduces transmission.
Exam answer essentials
  • If asked for the biological term for the mosquito’s role, write vector. “Carrier” may be insufficient. (2023 Higher, Q05.1.)

5. Non-specific body defences

  • Skin: a physical barrier to entry; a wound breaks the barrier.
  • Nose: hairs and mucus trap particles and pathogens.
  • Trachea and bronchi: mucus traps pathogens; cilia move the mucus towards the throat, where it is swallowed.
  • Stomach: hydrochloric acid kills many swallowed pathogens.
  • These defences are non-specific: they act against many pathogens, rather than recognising one particular antigen.
Exam answer essentials
  • Cilia move mucus; mucus traps pathogens. Keep their roles distinct.

6. White blood cells & the immune response

An antigen is a molecule recognised by the immune system, often on a pathogen’s surface. An antibody is a protein with a binding site complementary to a specific antigen.
  • Phagocytosis: some white blood cells engulf and digest pathogens.
  • Antibodies: lymphocytes produce specific antibodies that bind to antigens and help destroy the pathogen. Different antigens require different antibodies.
  • Antitoxins: neutralise toxins released by pathogens.
  • The first response takes time. Following exposure, memory cells can allow a faster response to the same antigen.
Exam answer essentials
  • The white blood cell produces the antibody; the antibody binds the antigen. Antibodies do not engulf pathogens.
  • For specificity, explain complementary binding-site and antigen shapes. “It is specific” alone may not explain why.

7. Plant infections & aphids

  • Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV): causes a mosaic pattern of discolouration on leaves. This reduces photosynthesis and therefore growth. It affects many plant species, including tomatoes.
  • Rose black spot: a fungus causes purple or black spots; leaves turn yellow and fall early. Less leaf area → less photosynthesis → reduced growth. Spores spread by wind and water.
  • Control black spot with fungicides or remove and destroy affected leaves, reducing the source of spores.
  • Aphids: insects damage plants by feeding on sap; they can also transmit plant pathogens. The insect pest and an infection it transmits are different problems.
  • Plants can be infected by viral, bacterial and fungal pathogens, as well as damaged by insects.
Exam answer essentials
  • Complete the causal link: less photosynthesis → less glucose available for respiration and making new biomass → reduced growth.
AQA 4.3.3.1 · Biology only; identification HT only

8. Plant symptoms & mineral deficiencies

  • Nitrate ions are needed to make amino acids and proteins. Deficiency limits new tissue growth, causing stunted growth.
  • Magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll. Deficiency causes chlorosis (yellow leaves), reducing light absorption and photosynthesis.
  • Possible disease signs: stunted growth, leaf spots, decay, abnormal growths, malformed stems/leaves, discolouration, or visible pests. A symptom alone does not always identify its cause.
  • HT only: identify a disease using a gardening manual or reliable website, laboratory testing, or a kit containing monoclonal antibodies. Compare several signs and test evidence.
Exam answer essentials
  • A mineral deficiency is not caused by a pathogen. Use nitrate → proteins and magnesium → chlorophyll; do not swap them.

9. Plant defence mechanisms

CategoryMechanism & purpose
PhysicalCellulose cell walls and a tough waxy cuticle resist pathogen entry; bark/dead outer cells form a barrier and fall off.
ChemicalAntibacterial chemicals inhibit bacteria; poisons deter herbivores.
MechanicalThorns and hairs deter feeding; leaves may droop or curl when touched; mimicry can deter animals.
Exam answer essentials
  • Name a defence, then link it to blocking entry, inhibiting pathogens or deterring herbivores.

10. Culturing microorganisms: practical recap

  • The full method and labelled diagrams are in Chapter 1: culturing microorganisms.
  • Use sterile nutrient agar and Petri dishes; sterilise an inoculating loop and work aseptically to avoid contamination. School cultures are incubated at 25°C to reduce the chance of growing harmful human pathogens.
  • Tape the lid at a few points, do not seal it all the way round; incubate the plate upside down and do not reopen it after growth.
  • For an antibiotic investigation, compare equal-size discs, bacterial culture, agar depth, incubation time and temperature. Use a suitable control disc without antibiotic.
  • A larger zone of inhibition indicates greater inhibition under the tested conditions. Calculate area using πr²; halve a measured diameter to get the radius.
Exam answer essentials
  • A clear zone shows inhibited bacterial growth, not that an antibiotic would definitely work best in a patient.
