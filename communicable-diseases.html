AQA 4.3.1.1

1. Pathogens: what causes infection?

Pathogens are microorganisms that cause infectious disease. A communicable disease can be passed between individuals.
  • There are four pathogen groups: bacteria, viruses, fungi and protists. They can infect animals or plants; the infected organism is the host.
  • Bacteria are living cells. They can reproduce rapidly inside the body and release toxins (poisons), which damage tissues.
  • Viruses reproduce inside living cells. Making new viruses damages the host cells.
  • Fungi: some infect plants or animals. Protists: some are parasites — organisms that live in or on a host and obtain resources from it.
Pathogen groupNamed AQA examples
BacteriaSalmonella; gonorrhoea
VirusesMeasles; HIV; tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)
FungiRose black spot
ProtistsMalaria
Exam wording
  • For bacteria, say “bacteria produce toxins that damage tissues”. For viruses, say “viruses reproduce inside cells, causing cell damage”.
AQA 4.3.1.1

2. Transmission: how infection spreads

Transmission means a pathogen passing from one host to another.
RouteHow it spreadsHow to reduce transmission
AirDroplets from coughing or sneezing are inhaled, e.g. measles.Cover coughs; isolate infected people to reduce contact.
Water / foodContaminated material is swallowed, e.g. Salmonella in food.Safe drinking water; wash hands before preparing food; cook food thoroughly.
Direct contactContact with an infected individual or infected body fluids, e.g. sexual transmission of gonorrhoea/HIV.Hygiene; condoms reduce exchange of infected fluids; do not share needles.
VectorsAnother organism carries the pathogen between hosts, e.g. a mosquito carries the malaria protist.Prevent bites and reduce vector numbers.
  • Isolation separates infected people from others. Vaccination makes more people immune, so fewer people can become infected and pass the pathogen on.
Exam wording
  • Match the precaution to the disease: action → less transfer of its pathogen. For food poisoning, explain handwashing before handling food; a vague “be hygienic” may miss the point.
AQA 4.3.1.2–3

3. Four human diseases

Measles · virus

  • Spread: inhaling droplets from coughs and sneezes.
  • Symptoms: fever and a red skin rash. Complications can be fatal.
  • Prevention: young children are vaccinated.

HIV · virus

  • Spread: sexual contact or infected body fluids, including blood from shared needles.
  • Effects: initial flu-like illness; the virus attacks immune cells and can lead to AIDS.
  • Control: antiretroviral drugs control viral reproduction. Condoms and sterile needles reduce transmission.

Salmonella · bacterium

  • Spread: contaminated food is swallowed; unhygienic food preparation transfers bacteria.
  • Symptoms: fever, abdominal cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea, caused by bacteria and their toxins.
  • Prevention: hygiene and thorough cooking; UK poultry are vaccinated.

Gonorrhoea · bacterium

  • Spread: sexual contact.
  • Symptoms: thick yellow/green discharge from the penis or vagina; pain when urinating.
  • Control: condoms reduce spread; antibiotics treat infection. Many strains are resistant to penicillin.
AIDS is late-stage HIV infection: the immune system is so damaged that it cannot effectively deal with other infections or cancers.
  • Why HIV makes other infections more dangerous: damage to immune cells → weaker defence against pathogens → other infections are harder to control.
  • Know the drug names: HIV → antiretroviral drugs; bacterial disease → suitable antibiotics. Antibiotics do not kill viruses.
Exam wording
  • HIV is the virus; AIDS is the late-stage condition. HIV is not transmitted by ordinary social contact.
AQA 4.3.1.5

4. Malaria: separate the pathogen and vector

The malaria pathogen is a protist. The mosquito is a vector — an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts.
  • The protist’s life cycle involves both humans and mosquitoes. An infected mosquito transfers the protist when it bites a person.
  • Malaria causes recurrent (repeated) episodes of fever and can be fatal.
  • Mosquito nets prevent bites → fewer protists enter people.
  • Prevent mosquito breeding, for example by removing stagnant water → fewer vectors → less transmission.
Exam wording
  • If asked for the mosquito’s role, write vector (2023 Higher Q05.1). The mosquito does not cause malaria by being a bacterium or virus.

5. Defences that stop pathogens entering

Non-specific defences act against many different pathogens; they do not target one particular antigen.
PartWhat it does
SkinForms a physical barrier. Blood clotting forms a scab over a wound, helping close a route of entry.
NoseNose hairs and mucus trap particles and pathogens.
Trachea and bronchiMucus traps pathogens. Tiny hair-like cilia move the mucus towards the throat, where it is swallowed.
StomachHydrochloric acid kills many swallowed pathogens.
Exam wording
  • Mucus traps; cilia move. Cilia do not produce antibodies or trap pathogens on their own.
AQA 4.3.1.6

6. If pathogens get inside: white blood cells

Antigens are molecules, often on a pathogen’s surface, that the immune system recognises. Antibodies are proteins that bind to specific antigens.
DefenceWhat happensUse these words
PhagocytosisA white blood cell surrounds and takes the pathogen inside; enzymes digest it.Engulf and digest pathogens
Antibody productionLymphocytes (a type of white blood cell) make antibodies with binding sites that fit a particular antigen. Binding helps the body destroy the pathogen.Specific; complementary; bind to antigens
Antitoxin productionWhite blood cells produce antitoxins, which counteract the toxins released by bacteria.Neutralise toxins
  • Why an antibody is specific: its binding-site shape is complementary to the shape of one particular antigen — it fits that antigen.
  • The first response takes time. After exposure, memory cells can enable faster production of the correct antibodies if the same pathogen returns. See Module 2: vaccination.
Exam wording
  • White blood cells make antibodies; antibodies bind antigens. Antibodies do not engulf pathogens. Antitoxins act on toxins, not by eating bacteria.

7. Plant infections and aphid damage

ExampleWhat happensSpread / control
Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)Mosaic patches of discolouration on leaves. Less photosynthesis means less glucose available for growth. Infects many species, including tomatoes.Infected sap can transfer on hands/tools. Clean tools and avoid transferring infected plant material.
Rose black spotPurple/black spots; leaves turn yellow and drop early. Reduced leaf area reduces photosynthesis and growth.A fungus; spores spread by wind/water. Use fungicide or remove and destroy affected leaves.
AphidsInsects feed on plant sap, removing resources and damaging plants. They can also transmit plant pathogens.An insect pest, rather than a microorganism.
  • Plants can be infected by viral, bacterial and fungal pathogens. Insect damage and mineral deficiencies are other possible causes of poor plant health.
Exam wording
  • Explain reduced growth as a chain: less photosynthesis → less glucose → less material for making new biomass and less respiration → reduced growth.

8. Mineral deficiencies and recognising disease

Mineral ionWhat the plant needs it forDeficiency effect
Nitrate ionsMaking amino acids, then proteins for growth.Less protein synthesis → stunted growth (smaller than expected).
Magnesium ionsMaking chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis.Chlorosis (yellow leaves) → less photosynthesis.
Exam wording
  • A deficiency means an essential substance is missing. It is not an infection caused by a pathogen. Remember nitrate → proteins; magnesium → chlorophyll.

Higher Tier only — detection and identification

  • Detect possible disease: look for stunted growth, leaf spots, decay (rot), abnormal growths, malformed stems/leaves, discolouration or visible pests.
  • Identify the cause: compare with a gardening manual/website; send an infected plant to a laboratory; or use a testing kit containing monoclonal antibodies (specific antibodies that bind a particular target).
  • A yellow leaf alone does not prove an infection: magnesium deficiency also causes yellowing. Use the pattern of symptoms and test evidence.

9. Plant defence responses

Defence typeExamplesHow it helps
PhysicalCellulose cell walls; tough waxy leaf cuticle; layers of dead outer cells, including bark, which fall off.Form barriers that resist entry of microorganisms.
ChemicalAntibacterial chemicals; poisons.Antibacterial chemicals inhibit bacteria. Poisons deter herbivores (plant-eating animals).
Mechanical adaptationsThorns/hairs; leaves that droop or curl when touched; mimicry.Deter feeding or make feeding difficult. Mimicry tricks animals, e.g. markings resembling eggs can discourage egg-laying.
Exam wording
  • Name the feature and its effect: “The tough waxy cuticle forms a barrier, making it harder for pathogens to enter.” Plants do not make human white blood cells.
AQA 4.1.1.6 · Required practical 2 · Biology only

10. RP2: testing antiseptics or antibiotics

Aseptic technique prevents contamination by unwanted microorganisms. A zone of inhibition is a clear area around a treatment disc with no visible bacterial growth.
  • Aim: compare the effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth using nutrient agar (a nutrient-containing jelly) in Petri dishes. Use a teacher-approved bacterial culture and supervised school procedures.
  • Method: follow all nine illustrated steps below. The AQA method starts with a prepared bacterial lawn (an even covering of bacteria); you need to understand how sterile equipment and brief lid opening prevent contamination.
RP2 method — text version of the nine illustrated steps
  1. Disinfect hands and the bench.
  2. Use sterile nutrient agar and Petri dishes.
  3. Start with a technician-prepared bacterial lawn; understand how an even spread is made aseptically.
  4. Soak equal-sized paper discs in the different treatments using equal liquid volumes.
  5. Use sterile forceps to place discs well apart. Lift the lid only briefly at an angle and replace it promptly.
  6. Include a disc soaked in the treatment’s solvent only as a control; label the treatments on the dish base.
  7. Secure the lid with two small pieces of tape, leaving gaps. Incubate inverted at 25°C for about 48 hours, following the teacher’s instructions.
  8. Keep the plate closed. Measure each clear zone in two perpendicular directions and calculate its mean diameter.
  9. Repeat treatments, compare mean results and identify anomalies.
ChoiceReason
Sterile agar/dishes and forcepsAvoid unwanted microorganisms affecting the results.
Partly tape the lidSecure it against accidental opening; gaps allow oxygen in. Do not seal all the way around.
Incubate inverted at 25°CInverting prevents condensation dripping onto agar. The lower temperature reduces the risk of growing harmful pathogens.
Solvent-only controlShows whether the solvent itself inhibits growth. Use the same solvent as the treatments.
Equal disc size, liquid volume and cultureMake differences in inhibition more likely to be due to the treatment.
  • The treatment diffuses from the paper disc into the agar. Where it prevents bacterial growth, a clear zone forms.
  • Measure without opening: measure each clear zone’s diameter in two perpendicular directions and calculate the mean. Repeat treatments on additional plates to assess variation.
  • Interpret: a larger zone suggests more inhibition under the same conditions. Differences in diffusion can also affect zone size, so this does not prove a treatment is always best.
  • Area if asked: radius = diameter ÷ 2; area = πr². Use mm² for area and mm for diameter. Compare means; explain anomalous results.
Exam wording
  • Use specific aseptic actions: disinfect the bench; sterilise the Petri dish/agar; use sterile forceps; open the lid only briefly. “Clean equipment” alone may be too vague. Never open an incubated plate.
