1. Magnification & resolution

  • Magnification is how many times larger an image is than the real object.
  • Resolution is the ability to distinguish two points close together as separate points.
Exam answer essentials
  • If asked why an electron microscope reveals more detail, explain its higher resolving power.
  • “It zooms in more” is insufficient for a resolution explanation.
  • A light microscope uses light and lenses.
  • An electron microscope has much higher magnification and resolving power, allowing finer detail in sub-cellular structures to be seen.
  • Improvements in microscopy have helped scientists understand cell structures.
Magnification = image size ÷ real size
  • Rearrange: real size = image size ÷ magnification; image size = real size × magnification.
  • Convert both sizes into the same units first.
  • Magnification has no unit.
Worked example
  • An image is 40 mm long at ×800 magnification.
  • Real size = 40 ÷ 800 = 0.050 mm = 50 µm.
  • Using a scale bar: real object length = (measured object length ÷ measured scale-bar length) × the real length represented by the bar.
  • If an object measures 20 mm and a 5 mm bar represents 10 µm, real object length = (20 ÷ 5) × 10 = 40 µm.
  • Measure the object and bar in the same units.
  • Total light-microscope magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification.
  • A ×10 eyepiece and ×40 objective give ×400.

2. Required practical 1: microscopy

Exam answer essentials
  • “Stain makes it easier to see” is better explained as increased contrast / making cell structures more visible.
  • A thin specimen allows light to pass through.
  • Lowering a coverslip at an angle reduces air bubbles.
AQA requirement: use a light microscope to observe, draw and label a selection of plant and animal cells. Include a magnification scale. The onion wet mount below is one preparation method; prepared slides may also be used.

Method, explained

  1. Use forceps to peel a thin epidermis from an onion. A thin layer allows light through and helps you see individual cells.
  2. Place the tissue flat in a drop of water on a clean slide. Avoid folds.
  3. Add a small amount of iodine stain to increase contrast and make structures easier to see.
  4. Lower a coverslip slowly at an angle using a mounted needle. This reduces trapped air bubbles.
  5. Secure the slide on the stage. Select the lowest-power objective, position the tissue above the light, and adjust illumination.
  6. Use coarse focus at low power, then fine focus to sharpen the image. Keep the objective from striking the slide.
  7. Centre the cells, increase objective power, and use fine focus to sharpen again.
  8. Observe a prepared animal-cell slide too. Draw and label the structures actually visible.

Biological drawings

  • Use a sharp pencil, clear single outlines, sensible proportions, and a large drawing. Avoid shading.
  • Use straight label lines ending on the correct structures. Give the specimen a title.
  • Record magnification. Add the magnification scale required by the activity; if a calibrated scale bar is used, label its real length. Never guess a real-size scale.
  • Light microscopes do not show all the detail in textbook diagrams. Onion bulb epidermis normally has no chloroplasts.

Safety: handle slides and coverslips carefully; iodine can irritate and stain, so follow the laboratory’s eye-protection guidance. Use a chopping board, cut away from the body, and keep fingers away from the blade. Use a dropper to control iodine and clean up spills; wash any skin contact immediately.

3. Culturing microorganisms

Exam answer essentials
  • Give the reason for the specific precaution.
  • “For safety” is too vague.
  • Sterilisation kills unwanted microorganisms; low incubation temperature reduces pathogen growth.
  • Name the equipment and method, such as passing an inoculating loop through a flame.
  • Washing alone is not sterilisation.
  • Bacteria reproduce by binary fission, dividing into two cells.
  • Under suitable conditions, division can occur as often as once every 20 minutes.
  • Growth depends on suitable temperature and available nutrients; the division time given in a question may differ.
  • Bacteria can be grown in a nutrient broth, or as colonies on nutrient agar.
  • Cultures allow scientists to investigate the effects of antiseptics or antibiotics on growth.
Number after growth = starting number × 2number of divisions
  • Number of divisions = total time ÷ division time, using the same time units.
  • For Higher Tier, express the final population in standard form when asked (for example, 640 = 6.4 × 102).
  • Example: 10 bacteria dividing every 20 minutes for 2 hours undergo 120 ÷ 20 = 6 divisions.
  • Final number = 10 × 26 = 640.
  • To prepare an uncontaminated culture, sterilise the medium and equipment, transfer the intended culture with a sterile cooled loop, briefly lift the lid to streak the agar, then replace and secure the lid.
  • These precautions prevent other organisms being introduced.

Aseptic technique: action → reason

ActionReason
Sterilise nutrient medium and Petri dishes before useKills unwanted microorganisms that could contaminate the culture.
Sterilise an inoculating loop using a flame; allow it to coolKills microorganisms on the loop; cooling avoids killing the intended culture.
Open the lid as little as possibleReduces contamination from microorganisms in the air.
Secure the lid with tape; do not fully seal itKeeps the lid in place and reduces contamination while allowing oxygen in; avoids conditions encouraging harmful anaerobic bacteria.
Incubate school cultures at a maximum of 25°CReduces the risk of growing human pathogens.
Store plates upside downPrevents condensation dripping onto the agar and spreading colonies.

4. Required practical 2: inhibition zones

Exam answer essentials
  • A larger zone shows greater inhibition under the tested conditions; it does not alone prove the best treatment for a patient.
  • Use radius in πr² and give area in mm².
  • Repeats help identify anomalies and calculate a more reliable mean.
  • The untreated disc is a control for comparison, not a control variable; it checks that inhibition is due to the treatment rather than the disc or solvent.
AQA requirement: investigate the effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth using agar plates, measuring zones of inhibition. This is a supervised school practical.

Method, explained

  1. Disinfect the workspace and clean hands. Use sterile equipment and nutrient agar inoculated with a suitable bacterial culture.
  2. Using sterile forceps, place discs containing the treatments onto the agar. Space them so their zones do not overlap.
  3. Add a control disc with the solvent alone, such as sterile water. It checks whether the solvent or disc affects bacterial growth.
  4. Label the base; secure the lid with tape without sealing the entire edge.
  5. Incubate inverted at a maximum of 25°C for the same period. Do not open the plate after incubation.
  6. Measure the diameter of each clear zone of inhibition. For an irregular zone, take two perpendicular diameters and calculate the mean. Repeat treatments on suitable replicate plates and compare means.
  • Independent variable: type or concentration of treatment.
  • Dependent variable: diameter or area of the clear zone.
  • Controls: bacterial species and starting culture, agar depth/composition, disc size, treatment volume, incubation time and temperature.
Area of a circular inhibition zone = πr²
  • For a diameter of 12 mm, radius = 6 mm; area = π × 6² ≈ 113 mm².
  • Follow the question if it specifies whether to include or exclude the disc area.
