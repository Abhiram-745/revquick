1. Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient.
Exam answer essentials
  • State the substance, its higher and lower concentrations, and direction.
  • A short diffusion distance also speeds exchange; do not confuse distance with surface area.
  • Diffusion applies to gas particles and dissolved substances.
  • It is passive: it does not require energy from respiration.
  • Particles move randomly in both directions, but more move from the higher-concentration side, producing a net movement.
  • Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion during gas exchange.
  • Urea, a waste substance, diffuses from cells into blood plasma for transport to the kidneys.
  • Some dissolved substances diffuse across cell membranes.
Factor increasedWhy diffusion is faster
Concentration differenceA steeper gradient produces greater net movement.

2. Surface area & exchange surfaces

Exam answer essentials
  • Explain how each adaptation increases exchange.
  • “Thin walls allow fast diffusion because the diffusion distance is short” is a complete link.
  • “Thin walls give a large surface area” confuses two different adaptations.
  • A single-celled organism has a large surface-area-to-volume ratio, so enough substances can diffuse across its surface to meet its needs.
  • As organisms get larger, volume increases faster than surface area: the ratio falls and internal diffusion distances become longer.
Surface-area-to-volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume

  • Leaves: a broad surface provides a large area; a thin leaf gives a short diffusion path.
  • Stomata allow gases to enter and leave, while internal air spaces allow gases to reach mesophyll cells.
  • Roots: root hairs give a large surface area; water enters by osmosis and mineral ions may enter by active transport.
  • Large multicellular organisms need specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems.
  • Be able to explain adaptations in the small intestine, lungs, fish gills, roots and leaves.

3. Osmosis

Osmosis is the net diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.
Exam answer essentials
  • Always say water, not “particles”.
  • Do not mix up water and solute concentration: water moves from higher water concentration to lower water concentration.
  • At equilibrium there is no net movement, not no movement at all.
  • A dilute solution has a higher water concentration; a more concentrated solution has a lower water concentration.
  • The membrane allows water molecules through but prevents some solute particles passing.

4. Required practical 3: osmosis

Exam answer essentials
  • Blotting removes surface solution that would otherwise alter measured mass.
  • Keep dimensions, time, temperature and volume controlled.
  • Explain mass loss as water leaving the cells by osmosis, rather than “sugar leaving the potato”.
AQA requirement: investigate how a range of salt or sugar solution concentrations affects the mass of plant tissue.

Method, explained

  1. Use a cork borer and scalpel to make potato cylinders of the same diameter and length. Follow safe cutting procedures.
  2. Blot each cylinder gently and consistently. Measure and record its initial mass using a balance.
  3. Place cylinders in a range of known salt or sugar concentrations, including distilled water. Use the same solution volume; keep the cylinders submerged.
  4. Leave them for the same time at the same temperature. Use tissue from the same potato/source to reduce biological variation.
  5. Remove, blot consistently to remove surface solution, and record final mass.
  6. Repeat for each concentration. Calculate each percentage mass change, identify justified anomalies, and calculate a mean.
Percentage mass change = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100
  • Example: initial mass 2.00 g; final mass 2.30 g.
  • Change = 0.30 g; percentage change = 0.30 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +15%.
  • A negative result means mass was lost.
  • Percentage change allows comparison when initial masses differ slightly.
  • Rate of water uptake: mass gained ÷ time.
  • If tissue gains 0.30 g in 30 minutes, mean uptake rate = 0.010 g/min.
  • A percentile is the value at or below which a given percentage of results lies: the 90th percentile has 90% of results at or below it.
  • Use the percentile method specified in the question.

Graph & interpretation

  • Put solution concentration on the x-axis and mean percentage mass change on the y-axis. Label quantities and units.
  • Use even scales, plot carefully, and draw an appropriate line or curve of best fit.
  • Positive change: net water enters the tissue. Negative change: net water leaves.
  • The concentration where the graph crosses 0% change estimates the tissue’s equivalent solution concentration: there is no net water movement.

5. Active transport

Exam answer essentials
  • To identify active transport, connect movement against the gradient with the energy requirement.
  • Cells doing much active transport often have many mitochondria.
Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against a concentration gradient, using energy released by respiration.
  • Membrane transport proteins help move the substances.
  • This lets cells take in useful substances even when their concentration outside is lower than inside.

Root hair cells

  • Absorb mineral ions from very dilute soil solutions into more concentrated cell contents.
  • The minerals are needed for healthy growth.

Small intestine

  • Glucose can be absorbed into blood even when its concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood.
  • Glucose is used in respiration.
