1. Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient.

Diffusion is passive: it does not require energy from respiration. Particles move randomly in both directions, but more move from the higher-concentration side, producing a net movement.

  • Oxygen and carbon dioxide move by diffusion during gas exchange.
  • Urea, a waste substance, diffuses from cells into blood plasma for transport to the kidneys.
  • Some dissolved substances diffuse across cell membranes.
Factor increasedWhy diffusion is faster
Concentration differenceA steeper gradient produces greater net movement.
TemperatureParticles have more kinetic energy and move faster.
Surface areaMore particles can cross at the same time.
Mark scheme focusState the substance, its higher and lower concentrations, and direction. A short diffusion distance also speeds exchange; do not confuse distance with surface area.

2. Surface area & exchange surfaces

When estimating the size of a structure in an image, compare it with a known scale or cell size. If a nucleus is about one-quarter the width of a 40 µm cell, estimate its width as 10 µm. Estimate areas using appropriate shapes, such as πr² for a roughly circular outline; state the simplification.

A single-celled organism has a large surface-area-to-volume ratio, so enough substances can diffuse across its surface to meet its needs. As organisms get larger, volume increases faster than surface area: the ratio falls and internal diffusion distances become longer.

Surface-area-to-volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume

3. Osmosis

Osmosis is the net diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.

A dilute solution has a higher water concentration; a more concentrated solution has a lower water concentration. The membrane allows water molecules through but prevents some solute particles passing.

Surroundings compared with cell contentsPlant cellAnimal cell
More diluteWater enters; vacuole expands; cell becomes turgid. The wall resists bursting.Water enters; cell swells and may burst.
Same concentrationNo net movement; no gain or loss of water overall.No net movement; size remains stable.
More concentratedWater leaves; cell becomes flaccid. With sufficient loss, the membrane pulls away from the wall: plasmolysis.Water leaves; cell shrinks.
Mark scheme focusAlways say water, not “particles”. Do not mix up water and solute concentration: water moves from higher water concentration to lower water concentration. At equilibrium there is no net movement, not no movement at all.

4. Required practical 3: osmosis

AQA requirement: investigate how a range of salt or sugar solution concentrations affects the mass of plant tissue.

Method, explained

  1. Use a cork borer and scalpel to make potato cylinders of the same diameter and length. Follow safe cutting procedures.
  2. Blot each cylinder gently and consistently. Measure and record its initial mass using a balance.
  3. Place cylinders in a range of known salt or sugar concentrations, including distilled water. Use the same solution volume; keep the cylinders submerged.
  4. Leave them for the same time at the same temperature. Use tissue from the same potato/source to reduce biological variation.
  5. Remove, blot consistently to remove surface solution, and record final mass.
  6. Repeat for each concentration. Calculate each percentage mass change, identify justified anomalies, and calculate a mean.
Percentage mass change = (final mass − initial mass) ÷ initial mass × 100

Example: initial mass 2.00 g; final mass 2.30 g. Change = 0.30 g; percentage change = 0.30 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +15%. A negative result means mass was lost. Percentage change allows comparison when initial masses differ slightly.

Rate of water uptake: mass gained ÷ time. If tissue gains 0.30 g in 30 minutes, mean uptake rate = 0.010 g/min. A percentile is the value at or below which a given percentage of results lies: the 90th percentile has 90% of results at or below it. Use the percentile method specified in the question.

Graph & interpretation

  • Put solution concentration on the x-axis and mean percentage mass change on the y-axis. Label quantities and units.
  • Use even scales, plot carefully, and draw an appropriate line or curve of best fit.
  • Positive change: net water enters the tissue. Negative change: net water leaves.
  • The concentration where the graph crosses 0% change estimates the tissue’s equivalent solution concentration: there is no net water movement.
Mark scheme focusBlotting removes surface solution that would otherwise alter measured mass. Keep dimensions, time, temperature and volume controlled. Explain mass loss as water leaving the cells by osmosis, rather than “sugar leaving the potato”. AQA June 2022 Paper 1 Higher, Q01.9, links water leaving, osmosis, and the more concentrated external solution.

5. Active transport

Active transport moves substances from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against a concentration gradient, using energy released by respiration.

Membrane transport proteins help move the substances. This lets cells take in useful substances even when their concentration outside is lower than inside.

Root hair cells

Absorb mineral ions from very dilute soil solutions into more concentrated cell contents. The minerals are needed for healthy growth.

Small intestine

Glucose can be absorbed into blood even when its concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. Glucose is used in respiration.

ProcessWhat moves?DirectionEnergy from respiration?
DiffusionParticlesHigher → lower concentrationNo
OsmosisWaterDilute → concentrated solution through a partially permeable membraneNo
Active transportSubstances such as glucose/mineral ionsLower → higher concentrationYes
Mark scheme focusTo identify active transport, connect movement against the gradient with the energy requirement. Cells doing much active transport often have many mitochondria.
