1. Plant tissues & leaf structure

Exam answer essentials
  • Use the tissue’s structure and function together: palisade cells contain many chloroplasts → absorb light for photosynthesis. Meristem cells divide for growth.
Tissue / structureFunction & adaptation
Epidermal tissueCovers and protects surfaces. A transparent upper epidermis lets light reach photosynthetic cells; a waxy cuticle reduces water loss.
Palisade mesophyllCells near the upper surface contain many chloroplasts → absorb light for photosynthesis.
Spongy mesophyllAir spaces allow gases to move through the leaf and reach cells.
Xylem & phloemTransport tissues in veins: xylem supplies water / mineral ions; phloem carries dissolved sugars.
Guard cells & stomataGuard cells change shape to control the stomatal pore → regulate gas exchange and water loss.
Meristem tissueAt growing root and shoot tips; dividing cells produce new cells which can differentiate.

2. Roots & absorption

Exam answer essentials
  • Water enters by osmosis; mineral ions may enter by active transport. Do not describe mineral-ion absorption as osmosis.
  • Root hair cells have long projections → large surface area for absorption.
  • Water enters by osmosis through a partially permeable membrane when the external solution is more dilute than the cell contents.
  • Mineral ions can enter from a lower concentration in soil into a higher concentration inside cells by active transport. This needs energy released by respiration.
  • Many mitochondria help supply energy for active transport. Roots use mineral ions for healthy growth.
  • Roots, stems and leaves form an organ system that supplies substances and transports products around the plant.

3. Xylem, phloem & translocation

Translocation is the movement of dissolved food molecules, especially sugars, through phloem to where they are used or stored.
Exam answer essentials
  • For a comparison, cover both structure and function. Match each point: dead / living; hollow / perforated end walls; water and mineral ions / dissolved sugars. (2021 Higher, Q06.6.)
FeatureXylemPhloem
CellsDead, hollow cells joined into tubes; no cytoplasm obstructing flow.Living elongated cells joined into tubes; pores in end walls let sap pass.
StrengthWalls strengthened by lignin; also support the plant.Transport tissue without the thick lignified walls of xylem.
ContentsWater and mineral ions.Dissolved sugars.
MovementFrom roots towards stems and leaves in the transpiration stream.From sources such as leaves to places of use or storage; can be up or down the plant.
  • Sugars may move to growing tissues for respiration or to storage organs. Their destination depends on the plant’s needs.
AQA 4.2.3.2

4. Transpiration & stomata

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mainly through the stomata in its leaves.
Exam answer essentials
  • Describe evaporation from mesophyll surfaces, then diffusion of water vapour out through stomata. Water does not leave the leaf as liquid by osmosis.

Method — what to do and why

  1. Water evaporates from moist mesophyll-cell surfaces into air spaces in the leaf.
  2. Water vapour diffuses through open stomata into the outside air, down a concentration gradient.
  3. This water loss draws more water along the xylem from the roots: the transpiration stream.
  4. Water entering roots replaces the water lost from leaves.
  • When guard cells become turgid, the pore opens; when they lose water and become less turgid, it closes.
  • Open stomata let carbon dioxide enter for photosynthesis but also allow water loss. Closing them conserves water but reduces gas exchange.
  • The transpiration stream supplies water for photosynthesis, transports mineral ions and helps cool the plant through evaporation.

5. Factors affecting transpiration

Exam answer essentials
  • For each factor, explain the mechanism, not just “the rate increases”. In comparisons, keep the other conditions the same.
ChangeUsual effectWhy
Higher temperatureIncreases transpiration.Faster evaporation and more rapid diffusion of water vapour.
Higher humidityDecreases transpiration.More water vapour outside the leaf → a smaller concentration gradient.
More air movement / windIncreases transpiration.Removes humid air from around the leaf → maintains a steep gradient.
Higher light intensityUsually increases transpiration.Stomata generally open in light for photosynthetic gas exchange.
  • These trends apply while other conditions are controlled. Severe water stress can close stomata and change the pattern.
  • Use graphs carefully: calculate a mean from repeats; a gradient gives a rate. For a changing rate, use a tangent at the point specified.
  • Compare stomatal density using several randomly selected equal-area fields of view. Density = number of stomata ÷ area; report an appropriate unit such as stomata/mm².

6. Potometer investigation & rate calculations

A potometer measures water uptake by a shoot. This estimates transpiration because most absorbed water replaces water lost from leaves.
Exam answer essentials
  • It measures water uptake, not water loss directly: some water is used in photosynthesis and growth. This is an apparatus investigation, not an extra numbered AQA required practical.

Method — six steps, with reasons

Adapted from Practical Biology and Pearson apparatus guidance; simplified for GCSE.

  1. Prepare: fill the apparatus with water. Cut and fit the leafy shoot under water to prevent air entering and blocking the xylem.
  2. Check: seal joins with petroleum jelly, check for leaks, clamp securely and gently dry the leaves. Keep the apparatus airtight and watertight.
  3. Settle: allow the shoot to adjust to the conditions. Briefly lift the open capillary tip out of water to introduce one bubble, then return it underwater.
  4. Measure: record the start position and use a ruler and stopwatch to measure bubble movement towards the shoot over a fixed time. Keep the tip underwater.
  5. Repeat: use the reservoir to reset the bubble; close the tap before measuring. Aim for three readings per condition and calculate a mean.
  6. Investigate: change one factor through several values. Allow adjustment each time; keep the shoot, tube, timing and other conditions the same.
  • Safety: use cutting tools and glassware carefully under supervision. Reset before the bubble reaches the shoot.

Why use a narrow capillary?

Two linked exam points
  • The bubble moves further in a given time for the same water uptake, so resolution is improved — a small change in volume is easier to detect.
  • A smaller cross-sectional area gives a larger distance for the same volume. It does not increase the plant’s transpiration rate.

Planning another investigation

Change one factorHow?Keep the test fair
Air movementVary fan speed or distance.Keep light, temperature and humidity constant.
Light intensityVary lamp distance; measure light if possible.Use a heat shield and monitor temperature; keep humidity and air movement constant.
HumidityCompare normal air with humid air inside a clear bag.Keep temperature and light similar; the bag can also reduce air movement, so consider this limitation.
  • Use the same shoot to control leaf area. Allow it to adjust after each change; repeat measurements and compare mean rates.
Distance rate = bubble distance ÷ time
  • 24 mm in 4 min → 24 ÷ 4 = 6 mm/min. Use consistent units.
  • Volume rate = cross-sectional area × distance rate. Use the area given in the question, or calculate it using πr² (r = internal radius).
Volume calculation example

r = 0.5 mm; distance = 24 mm; time = 4 min.
Volume = π × 0.5² × 24 = 18.85 mm³.
Volume rate = 18.85 ÷ 4 = 4.71 mm³/min.
