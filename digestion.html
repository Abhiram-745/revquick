1. Cells → tissues → organs → systems

Tissue: a group of cells with a similar structure and function.
Exam answer essentials
  • A tissue answer needs similar cells / a shared function; “a group of cells” alone is incomplete. (June 2023 Higher, Q01.1.)

  • In the stomach, muscle tissue churns food, glandular tissue secretes digestive juices, and epithelial tissue covers surfaces.
  • An organ contains several types of tissue; it is not simply a larger tissue.

2. Digestion & absorption

Digestion breaks large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules. Absorption moves the products through the intestinal wall into the circulation.
Exam answer essentials
  • Link the process to its purpose: smaller, soluble molecules can be absorbed. Digestion and absorption are different processes.
  • Mouth: teeth break food up; saliva contains amylase.
  • Oesophagus: muscular contractions move food to the stomach.
  • Stomach: churns food and secretes protease and hydrochloric acid; acid provides suitable conditions for stomach protease and kills many swallowed microorganisms.
  • Small intestine: digestion is completed and nutrients are absorbed. Villi provide a large surface area; their thin surface gives a short diffusion distance, and a good blood supply maintains a concentration gradient.
  • Large intestine: absorbs water from the remaining material. Undigested material is stored in the rectum and leaves through the anus.
  • Pancreas: secretes digestive enzymes into the small intestine. The liver supplies bile, stored in the gall bladder.
AQA 4.2.2.1

3. Enzymes & the lock-and-key model

An enzyme is a biological catalyst: it speeds up a reaction without being used up. Enzymes are proteins.
Exam answer essentials
  • Use complementary shapes and explain that the substrate binds at the active site. “They fit” alone can be too vague. (2021 Higher, Q05.3.)
  • The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on. It binds to an active site with a complementary shape.
  • An enzyme–substrate complex forms. The reaction occurs, products leave, and the unchanged enzyme can be used again.
  • An enzyme catalyses a specific reaction because only suitable substrates bind to its active site.
  • The lock-and-key model is a simplified explanation: it emphasises the relationship between substrate and active-site shapes.
  • Metabolism means all the chemical reactions in a cell or organism. Enzymes control many of these reactions, including digestion and building larger molecules.

4. Temperature, pH & denaturation

Denaturation: a change in an enzyme’s shape, including its active site, so the substrate no longer fits and the reaction slows or stops.
Exam answer essentials
  • For high temperature: active site changes shape → substrate no longer fits → fewer enzyme–substrate complexes. Enzymes are not alive, so do not say they are “killed”. (2021 Higher, Q05.7.)
ConditionExplanation
Temperature rising below the optimumParticles gain kinetic energy → more frequent successful collisions → reaction rate increases.
Optimum temperatureThe enzyme’s highest reaction rate under the tested conditions.
High temperature above the optimumThe protein structure changes; the active site loses its complementary shape → rate decreases.
Low temperatureLess kinetic energy and fewer successful collisions; the enzyme is usually not denatured.
pHEach enzyme has an optimum pH. Moving away from it reduces activity; sufficiently extreme pH can change the active-site shape.
  • Stomach protease works in acidic conditions; enzymes in the small intestine generally work best in neutral or alkaline conditions.
  • Read an optimum from the graph or data supplied. Do not assume every enzyme has the same optimum.
  • A graph of rate peaks at the optimum; a graph of time taken has its minimum there, because faster reactions finish sooner.

5. Digestive enzymes, bile & food products

Exam answer essentials
  • Name the production site accurately: “salivary glands”, not just “mouth”; specify small intestine. (2021 Higher, Q05.2.)
  • Bile emulsifies fat; it does not chemically digest it. Link smaller droplets to more surface area for lipase.
EnzymeProduced inWord equation
Amylase (a carbohydrase)Salivary glands, pancreas, small intestineStarch → sugars
ProteasesStomach, pancreas, small intestineProteins → amino acids
LipasesPancreas, small intestineLipids → glycerol + fatty acids
  • Carbohydrases break carbohydrates into simple sugars; amylase acts on starch. You do not need chemical-symbol equations.
  • Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder and released into the small intestine.
  • Bile is alkaline → neutralises acid arriving from the stomach → gives suitable conditions for intestinal enzymes.
  • Bile breaks large fat globules into small droplets → greater total surface area → lipase breaks fat down faster.
  • Digested molecules are absorbed and used to build new carbohydrates, lipids and proteins. Some glucose is used in respiration to release energy.

6. Required practical 4: food tests

Exam answer essentials
  • Give reagent + method + positive colour/result. “It changes colour” is incomplete. Benedict’s tests reducing sugars, not starch or all carbohydrates.

Method — what to do and why

  1. Prepare a food sample: crush a small amount with distilled water, mix and filter if needed. Use fresh, labelled portions for each test.
  2. Reducing sugars: add Benedict’s solution and heat in a hot water bath. A positive result changes blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate, depending on the amount present. A negative result stays blue.
  3. Starch: add iodine solution. Positive: blue-black. Negative: orange-brown. No heating is required.
  4. Protein: add Biuret reagent and mix. Positive: purple / lilac. Negative: blue.
  5. Lipids — Sudan III method: add Sudan III and shake gently. A positive result has a red-stained oil layer.
  6. Alternative lipid test — ethanol emulsion: shake the sample with ethanol, then add the extract to water. A milky-white emulsion indicates lipid; a negative result remains clear.

Reliable results & safety

  • Use separate pipettes / clean glassware to avoid contamination. Compare with distilled water as a negative control; a known positive sample checks the test works.
  • Wear eye protection. Use a water bath carefully and point tubes away from people. Ethanol is flammable: keep it away from flames.
  • These are qualitative tests: they identify substances present. A Benedict’s colour can suggest an amount, but is not a precise concentration measurement without calibration.
AQA 4.2.2.1 · RP5

7. Required practical 5: pH & amylase

Exam answer essentials
  • The endpoint is no starch remaining: iodine stays orange-brown. Iodine detects starch, not sugar.
  • For a pH investigation, use a buffer and keep temperature constant. Apply controls to the actual factor being investigated.

Method — what to do and why

  1. Place iodine drops in separate wells of a spotting tile.
  2. Put measured volumes of starch solution and amylase solution, with the selected pH buffer, in a water bath. Let them reach the controlled temperature before mixing.
  3. Combine the solutions, mix and start the timer immediately. Keep the reaction tube in the water bath.
  4. Take a drop of reaction mixture at the start, then every 30 seconds. Add each drop to a fresh iodine well. Blue-black means starch is still present.
  5. Record the first time a sample leaves iodine orange-brown. Starch has been broken down.
  6. Repeat at several pH values. Repeat each condition and calculate a mean, investigating anomalies.

Variables & interpretation

  • Independent: pH of the reaction mixture.
  • Dependent: time until starch is no longer detected; use this to calculate relative rate.
  • Controls: temperature; volumes and concentrations of amylase and starch; buffer volume; sample volume and sampling interval.
  • If starch is not fully digested within the observation period, record that rather than treating the reaction as completed.
  • Thirty-second sampling gives an interval estimate of the endpoint. More frequent sampling can improve time resolution.
Relative rate = 1 ÷ time taken
  • If completion takes 120 s, rate = 1 ÷ 120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹.
  • Plot pH on the x-axis and mean rate on the y-axis. The highest rate indicates the optimum pH.
  • Wear goggles; avoid skin contact with reagents and take care with hot water.
